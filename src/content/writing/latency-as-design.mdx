Latency usually shows up late — as a graph, an alert, a postmortem. By then it's a constraint
you're fighting. But if you decide early where your time budget goes, latency becomes
something you design with rather than around.

## A budget, spent on purpose

Say you have 200 milliseconds before an interaction feels sluggish. That's a budget. You can
spend it on a round trip, on rendering, on an animation that makes a wait feel intentional —
but you can only spend it once.

Naming that budget out loud, early, forces the important questions to the front:

- What has to be instant, and what can be optimistic?
- Where can we show something true before we show everything?
- Which waits should be hidden, and which should be honest?

## Time shapes interface

When you treat the budget as fixed, the interface starts to design itself. Optimistic updates,
skeleton states, streamed results, and progressive disclosure stop being tricks and become
consequences of a decision you already made.

The teams that ship the most responsive-feeling software aren't necessarily the fastest. They
decided, up front, what fast was going to mean.
