We tend to treat clarity as a cosmetic layer — something you apply once the real work is
done. Pick a nicer font, tidy the spacing, ship it. But in most systems I've worked on, the
place software actually breaks down is the moment a human tries to read its state and decide
what to do next.

## The reading moment

Every interface has a reading moment: the incident dashboard at 3am, the migration log
scrolling past, the settings page someone opens once a year and has to fully trust. If that
moment is confusing, no amount of backend correctness rescues it.

Legibility is the property that the system's state can be understood by the person who needs
to act on it, quickly and without folklore. It is not decoration. It is whether the software
can be operated.

## Designing it in

A few habits that make legibility structural rather than superficial:

- **A small vocabulary of states.** If something can be in seven subtly different conditions,
  people will collapse them into two anyway — badly. Choose the two or three that matter.
- **Stable layout under change.** Content that reflows while you read it destroys trust.
  Reserve space; stream into it.
- **Names that survive.** The clearest system is the one whose worst name is still good.

Clarity compounds. Every time you make state easier to read, you make every future decision
on top of it cheaper.
